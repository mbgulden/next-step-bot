## 2.3 How to Honor Their Decision-Making Styles

### For Your Style (JT) — The Gut-Response Engine

Your engine works by responding to specific, concrete stimuli. Abstract questions don't engage it. "What should we do with our lives?" — nothing. "Want to walk down to the river at 3 PM?" — boom, immediate yes or no.

**What works for your system:**
- Someone puts something specific in front of you. A concrete option. A defined proposal. A question with edges.
- Your body responds — a lean forward (yes) or a pull back (no), a sound (the "uh-huh" or "unh-unh"), a physical opening or closing.
- You trust that response, even if it makes no logical sense in the moment.
- You act on the yes; you decline the no.

**What doesn't work for your system:**
- Open-ended questions. "What do you want to do?" is a void your engine can't fire on.
- Being asked to initiate from nothing. Your power doesn't come from the idea — it comes from the response to the idea.
- Being asked to explain WHY your gut said yes or no. Your gut doesn't speak in reasons. It speaks in movement.

**How Jonathan can work with your style:**
- Instead of "What should we do for dinner?", say "Hey, want to grab dinner at 7 at that new Thai place?"
- Instead of "What do you think about our relationship?", say "I've been feeling more distant this week. Want to talk about it tonight after the kids are asleep?"
- Instead of "What's your vision for the next year?", say "Here are three directions I see for us. Option A... Option B... Option C... What does your body say?"

If Jonathan is reading this (or you're sharing it with him): the gift you can give JT is specificity. Give him something to respond TO. Not an open field. Not a philosophical question. A door he can walk through or not walk through.

The one that comes after he's slept on it, after he's felt it in different moods, after the wave has crested and settled — THAT one is truth.

## 2.4 The Conflict Feedback Loop & Circuit-Breaker

### The Loop

Here's what structurally happens when you and Jonathan get into conflict. Read this and see if you recognize the pattern:

1. **Trigger:** Jonathan feels emotionally unsettled about something — a decision, a dynamic between you, an external stressor. His emotional weather system starts building pressure.

2. **Absorption:** Your system (which runs on an open emotional receiver) immediately picks up Jonathan's emotional state. You feel unsettled and you don't know why — or more precisely, you think you know why (something must be wrong between you two) but what you're actually feeling is Jonathan's wave, not your own signal.

3. **Gut-Fire:** Your gut-response engine, now flooded with absorbed emotional tension, fires a "fix this now" response. Your body wants resolution. You push toward action, toward an answer, toward closing the loop.

4. **Pressure:** Jonathan, whose wave hasn't settled yet, experiences your push as pressure before he's ready. His system says "I'm not clear yet and now I'm being asked to perform clarity I don't have."

5. **Withdrawal:** Jonathan pulls back. Not because he doesn't care — because his system needs space to complete its process, and the pressure is interfering.

6. **Rejection Signal:** Your system reads Jonathan's withdrawal as rejection. "He's pulling away. Something IS wrong. I need to fix it."

7. **Escalation:** You push harder. He withdraws further. The loop tightens.

8. **Spiral:** You're both now operating from your stress warning signals — you from frustration (pushing and not getting the resolution your body wants), Jonathan from bitterness (being pushed before his clarity arrived and feeling unseen in his process).

### The Circuit-Breaker

The loop has a single exit point, and it's yours, JT. You own the circuit-breaker because you're the one whose engine fires the "fix it now" impulse. Jonathan cannot break the loop from his side — his withdrawal IS his system's self-protection. It's not a strategy; it's autonomic.

Here's the circuit-breaker script:

> **"I feel like I'm pushing. I'm going to step back. You know where to find me when you're ready."**

That's it. No blame. No "you always withdraw." No "fine, I'll just wait." Just structural recognition delivered without emotional charge.

What this does:
- It names the dynamic ("I'm pushing") without making Jonathan wrong for receiving it as pressure.
- It removes the pressure ("I'm going to step back") which is the exact thing Jonathan's system needs.
- It keeps the door open ("you know where to find me") which addresses your system's fear that stepping back equals abandonment.
- It puts the timeline in Jonathan's hands ("when you're ready") which is the only way his system can produce genuine clarity.

The hard part: you have to actually step back. Not step back while vibrating at a frequency of "come back come back come back." Real stepping back. Go do something physical. Use your engine on something else. Let his weather clear.

The gift: when Jonathan returns, he returns with real clarity, not weather. And your engine gets to respond to truth instead of noise. That's a better loop.

## 2.5 Three Concrete Experiments

These are not principles to understand. They are practices to DO. Start with one. Master it. Add the next.

### Experiment 1: The 24-Hour Rule

**The rule:** For any decision that affects both of you, JT proposes it, and Jonathan has a minimum of 24 hours before answering. JT does not ask for updates during the waiting period. No check-ins. No "just wondering if you've thought about it." No body language that says "I'm waiting." Twenty-four hours of clean space.

**Why it works:** It gives Jonathan's emotional weather system the minimum runway to produce clarity. It gives your system practice in trusting that resolution will come without your intervention. It interrupts the loop before it starts.

**How to start:**
- Pick one category of decisions this week: meals, weekend plans, household purchases, whatever. Apply the 24-hour rule to just that category.
- When you propose something, explicitly say: "24-hour rule on this one. Let me know tomorrow."
- When tomorrow comes and he gives you an answer, respond with your gut. If it's a yes from him and a yes from your body, move. If it's a yes from him and a no from your body, say: "My body is saying no to this. Can we find another way?" (See Experiment 2.)

**What you'll notice:** The first few times will feel uncomfortable. Your system wants resolution now. Sit in the discomfort. That's your growth edge, not a sign the experiment isn't working.

### Experiment 2: The Gut-Translation

**The rule:** When your body gives you a clear no about something Jonathan wants, you don't just say "no." You translate the no into language that separates the decision from the relationship.

**The script:** "My body is saying no to this specific thing — it's not a no to you. Can we find another way?"

**Why it works:** Your no is binary and physical. To Jonathan, whose system processes through emotional connection, an unexplained no can feel like rejection of him, not just rejection of the thing. By explicitly separating the no from the person, you give his system the data it needs to not personalize your body's response.

**Variations:**
- "My gut is closing on this option. Let's find a different door."
- "This specific thing isn't a match for me. What else could work?"
- "My body is pulling back from this approach. I'm still in with you."

**What you'll notice:** The first time you do this, Jonathan may still have an emotional reaction — that's his weather system doing its thing. But over time, the explicit "not a no to you" framing will train both your systems that your gut-response is about decisions, not about love.

### Experiment 3: The Recognition Deposit

**The rule:** Once a day, Jonathan tells you one specific thing he noticed you do well. You write it down somewhere visible.

**Why it works:** This feeds both of your engines simultaneously.

Your engine runs on response. When Jonathan recognizes you — when he SEES you doing your thing well and names it — your system gets the specific, concrete stimulus it needs to experience satisfaction. "He saw me handle that. He named it. I'm seen."

Jonathan's engine runs on recognition. He's built to see people clearly and invite them into their next expression. When he gives you a recognition deposit, he's exercising his natural design in a way that produces success for him and satisfaction for you. The loop is mutually reinforcing.

**How to start:**
- Pick a time of day that's consistent. Breakfast. Dinner. Before bed. Doesn't matter — consistency matters.
- Jonathan's job: one specific observation. Not "you're great." That's too abstract. "The way you talked to the contractor this morning — you were direct without being aggressive. That was impressive." Specific. Observed. Named.
- Your job: write it down. A notebook. A whiteboard. A note on your phone. Somewhere you'll see it.
- Your additional job: let it land. Don't deflect. Don't say "it was nothing." Receive the recognition. Let your body respond to it.

**What you'll notice:** Your nervous system will start looking forward to this. You'll start being more aware during the day of what Jonathan might notice. This creates a positive feedback loop instead of the conflict loop.
